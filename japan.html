I. Introduction

2026 is Japan time. After having been back to Asia in 2025 for the first time in over 13 years with my mongolian-chinese trip, we decided this time to go one step further and explore the land of the rising sun. Since this was our first time in the country, we opted for the classic, “introductory” Japanese itinerary that consisted in the following stops: Tokyo, Hakone, Lake Kawaguchi, Kyoto, Nara and Osaka before flying to tropical Japan in the Okinawa prefecture. Last but not least, we visited the capital of Taiwan, Taipei, where we ended our journey and sadly also our 2026 summer.

II. Jomon people

So where are Japanese people originally coming from? To make it really simple, we have to go back almost 40.000 years ago when the first migrations came from China, Korea and south-east Asia. During a more recent Japanese era, civilization started to spread and the so-called Jomon culture flourished (literally 'cord-marked' because of the regular pattern over various artifacts and pottery). Jomon people lived in pit dwellings and they were mostly living off fishing and plant foraging. They looked very different from actual Japanese and also probably looked very different between themselves. Between 1000 and 300 BCE another wave of migration from mainland Asia strongly shaped Japanese population, bringing extra technological skills and tools and creating a more stratified, slightly more advanced society. This was the beginning of the Yayoi period, a period that brought to Japan a huge amount of progress in many different ways. Jomon people can be seen as the ancestors of Ainu, a group of people from Hokkaido and the northern part of Japan from the XII century, representing a distinctive minority from the rest of the population, the Yamato people, that include 98% of modern Japan. Jomon/Ainu are considered one of the oldest populations in the archipelago, and during the imperial period they had to shrink their living space drastically because they were forced to live in reserves and nowadays their population is made only of tens of thousand individuals.

III. Shinto and Kami

"Everything is a God, you included". I was talking with the lady in charge of the accommodation where I stayed in Hakone about the strong propensity for phallic statues I believe Japanese culture has. You might ask yourself: what do I, phallic statues and God have in common? The answer is what I think she was trying to tell me. In Shintoism everything and everyone is inhabited by a sacred energy called kami, a sort of living decentralized God that makes everything alive including me, you, phallic statues and the lady at the counter I talked to. Shintoism is the indigenous religion of Japan and it is nowadays still widely practiced. It has been introduced to the country during the Yayoi period in 300 BC and in its core it focuses on harmony with nature, respect for ancestors and purity (that could also explain the obsession Japanese people have for cleanliness). Another vital element of a shintoist shrine is the torii gate (literally bird perch), the main entrance, the gate that perfectly represents Japan and can be seen everywhere, including mountain paths, forests and villages. It marks a sort of imaginary door between the ordinary and the extraordinary world (Ke/Hare) and purity can be also obtained by simply walking under it and practising a worship routine.

IV. The Fujiyama Hotel

Our short stay in Hakone was mostly steeped into a certain grey, rainy and foggy vibe that made that part of Japan a bit more mysterious than the usual. The small and busy roads that connect this sort of natural cradle that sits inside a former vulcano with the rest of the world, are shaped like some of the roads in the Italian Alps and that made me feel like I was in a strange but well-known place. Our room was located exactly over one of those roads and just in front of it, there is a charming, classy and historical hotel called the Fujiya hotel. The place was built in 1891, part of an era when Japan wanted to look like its western counterpart and one of the way to do it, was to build a western-look-alike hotel in the middle of a thermal town. Although we knew the price of the hotel was somehow prohibitive, we decided to venture inside and after having crossed a beautiful Japanese garden, we entered the property. Between the famous people that had the luck to sleep in this iconic place there are John Lennon & family, Albert Einstein, Chiang Kai-shek and I believe many other important people. Once inside we decided to head to the dark, wooden, half illuminated and half empty bar where we could breath-in all the atmosphere the place can offer while sipping a whisky sour meticulously made by the bartender. While sitting in this sort of Hopper painting scene, the first analogy that came into my mind, was the resemblance that the place has with the Gran Hotel Bolivar of Lima, where people can experience an extraordinary Pisco sour in a place that saw Ernest Hemingway, Orson Wells, the Rolling Stones and an endless number of celebrities sitting at the same bar.

V. An evolution from Mahayana: Zen Buddhism

Mahayana Buddhism is translated as the great vehicle, the widest branch of Buddhism in the world that covers almost 50% of all practitioners. It started in India around 1BCE and spread to eastern Asia, due to its versatility, its capacity to integrate itself with other philosophies such as Shintoism and the presence of certain infrastructures like the Silk road that obviously facilitated cultural exchanges between different cultures. In its core ideas, there are certain concepts like the one of Bodhisattva, the representation of the ultimate savior that anyone should aim to be, the last sailor to leave the boat, after all the humans have been saved. In Mahayana all things are interconnected to themselves and things only work in function of what it is next to them. This concept is called Sunyata and it is another core idea of Mahayana. Nirvana is there and it is accessible to everyone and in order to reach it, individuals should only focus on the entire universe full potential and not on themselves only. Basically either all or none. While in Japan I noticed a massive presence of Zen temples and in particular I was particularly intrigued by a little doll I spot in different sites called Daruma. The Daruma dolls are the depiction of the monk Bodhidharma, the founder of zen. Bodhidharma is a mysterious Indian-born figure, mostly famous for its intense non-stop zen practices that according to the legend lasted up to 9 years and included an eyelid cut off in order not to fall asleep during his practicing and lose the whole purpose. Darumas are paper round doll of different colors but mostly red that simply do not fall on one side because of their round base (okiagari). Initially the dolls are left with blank eyes and only one eye, the left, has to be painted with the intention of having a clear goal in mind. The second eye can eventually be painted one year after only if the target goal is reached and at the end of its annual circle, Darumas have to be burned. While in Osaka, I had the time to visit one of the Zen-Buddhist temple covered with Daruma dolls all over the place called Katsuo-ji. The temple is located in the middle of the mountains and to explore it all, it requires around one hour. The way locals express their general wish or a wish for an upcoming victory of different kind can be noticed although the area is pretty popular between tourist but visiting the temple is definitely a way to sparks curiosity about the subject.
