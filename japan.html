I. Introduction

2026 is Japan time. After having been back to Asia in 2025 for the first time in over 13 years with my mongolian-chinese trip, we decided this time to go one step further and explore the land of the rising sun. Since this was our first time in the country, we opted for the classic, “introductory” Japanese itinerary that consisted in the following stops: Tokyo, Hakone, Lake Kawaguchi, Kyoto, Nara and Osaka before flying to tropical Japan in the Okinawa prefecture. Last but not least, we visited the capital of Taiwan, Taipei, where we ended our journey and sadly also our 2026 summer.

II. Jomon people

So where are Japanese people originally coming from? To make it really simple, we have to go back almost 40.000 years ago when the first migrations came from China, Korea and south-east Asia to the archipelago. Their descendants formed the Jōmon civilisation, whose culture is sometimes described as one of the most ancient in the world.

During a more recent Japanese era, civilisation started to spread and the so-called Jōmon culture flourished (literally “cord-marked” because of the regular pattern over various ceremonial objects). Jōmon people lived in pit dwellings and they were mostly living of fishing and plant foraging. They looked very different from present-day Japanese people and probably also very different from each other, which suggests a very diverse genetic and cultural background. Between 1000 and 300 BCE another wave of migration from mainland Asia strongly shaped Japanese population, bringing extra technological skills and tools and creating a more stratified society.

This was the beginning of the Yayoi period, a period that brought to Japan a huge amount of progress under many different ways. Jōmon people can be seen as the ancestors of Ainu, who remained in the northern part of Japan and represented a distinctive minority within the wider population, while the Yamato people—who include 98% of modern Japan—expanded and became the dominant group.

In the imperial period, the Ainu had to shrink their living space drastically because they were forced to live in reserves. Today their population is made only of tens of thousands of individuals, still struggling to preserve their cultural identity in a nation that historically assimilated them.

III. Shinto and Kami

“Everything is a God, you included.” I was talking with the lady in charge of the accommodation where I stayed in Hakone about the strong propensity for phallic statues I believe Japan has, and she said: “Everything is a God, you included.” This sentence captures the spirit of Shinto, the indigenous religion of Japan, where everything and everyone is inhabited by sacred presences called kami.

You might ask yourself: what do I, phallic statues and God have in common? The answer is what I think she was trying to tell me. In Shintoism everything and everyone is inhabited by a living, decentralized sacredness — the “living decentralized God” that makes everything alive, including me, you, phallic statues and the lady at the counter I talked to.

Shinto is the indigenous religion of Japan and it is still widely practiced today. It was introduced to the country during the Yayoi period in 300 BCE and in its core it focuses on purification, ritual, and respect for nature. Japanese aesthetics, architecture and even cleanliness rituals deeply reflect this worldview, where a sacred dimension is perceived everywhere.

Another vital element of a Shinto shrine is the torii gate, the main entrance, the gate that perfectly represents Japan and can be seen everywhere, including in temple complexes and city landscapes. It acts as a sort of imaginary door between the ordinary and the extraordinary world (Ke/Hare), while purity can be obtained by simply walking under it and practising a worship routine.
